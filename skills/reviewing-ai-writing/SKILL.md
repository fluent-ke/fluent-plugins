# Reviewing AI Writing

## Two jobs, and they pull against each other

**Take out what makes writing read as machine-made. Leave the writer sounding like themselves.**

Either one alone is easy and useless. Strip every tell and you get competent, characterless prose — which is its own kind of machine writing. Protect the voice and change nothing and the tells ship.

The skill is holding both at once.

## Two modes

**Review** (default when asked whether something reads as AI, or to check a draft). Name each tell, quote the line, give the fix in a few words. Do not rewrite. Do not score the draft out of ten, and do not guess whether AI wrote it — detectors guess, and named patterns are evidence the writer can check for themselves. Offer to edit afterwards.

**Edit** (when handed a draft to fix). Make the **minimum effective change**. Fix tells, errors and genuinely unclear passages. Leave strong human sentences alone. A rough draft with a real voice should still sound like the same person afterwards. Return the draft plus a short note of what changed and why.

## Before you touch anything: find the voice

Read the whole thing first and note three to five markers that are *this writer's*: their vocabulary, their rhythm, their bluntness or hedging, their humour, their digressions, how polished they are. Keep that note to yourself and protect those things.

This is not decoration. The most common way this job goes wrong is not missing a tell — it is sanding a person down into something smooth. **Keep real hedges** ("I think", "maybe", "honestly") when they carry actual uncertainty or the writer's speaking rhythm. Keep the strong opinions, the swearing, the self-interruptions, the admissions. Keep sentences of uneven length.

## The one rule that governs everything

**A tell is a shape, not a line. Remove the shape or it grows back in the same pass.**

This is the observed failure, not a theory. Given a draft to improve, an unaided model reliably deletes "In conclusion" — and writes a negation reframe in its place. It removes "Here's the thing" and opens the next paragraph with "Here's the detail that changes everything:". It cuts an unfilled placeholder and adds two new ones.

Every one of those edits looks like progress and leaves the draft in the same condition.

So: when you cut a tell, **check what you wrote instead.** And **density is the signal** — one instance proves nothing, but three in a passage means the passage gets rewritten rather than edited.

## The tells that survive an ordinary edit

**These are the priority.** An ordinary careful edit already catches the word-level stuff further down; it reliably misses everything in this section, and often defends it.

### Negation reframe
"This isn't a tooling problem. It's a thinking problem." · "The question isn't X, it's Y." · "Not a stunt. Not a rebrand. A rebuild." · "It's not just X but Y."

**It works in both directions.** "This is a thinking problem, not a tooling problem" is the same shape with the halves swapped, and it is the form an edit reaches for while believing it has removed the tell. Naming the thing you are not is the tell, whichever end of the sentence it sits at.

The most persistent tell there is. Say the positive thing once, plainly.

**Watch for it in your own replacement.** This shape is where an unaided edit lands *by default* when it removes some other tell. If you cut a summary ending, read what you put there.

### Invented scene
"It's 9pm on a Tuesday and Amina is still at her desk…" · "Imagine a developer who…" · "A customer walks into your branch…"

Something that did not happen, written to manufacture relatability. Specific street names and a plausible time do not make it real — they make it a better-dressed invention.

**You will want to keep this one.** It reads well and it is usually the most vivid thing in the draft. Vivid and fabricated is still fabricated. Open from something that actually happened: a number, an event, a thing someone said.

### Three-beat cadence
"They test. They measure. They ship." · "The A. The B. The C."

Delete two. If one statement does not land, three will not — and rewriting three short beats as three long ones keeps the shape.

### Colon reveal
"The detail that changes everything: nobody owns it." · "The best part: it learns."

A noun phrase, a colon, then a small dramatic unveiling. Rewrite as a plain sentence. Colons are for lists, labels and quotes.

### Throat-clearing opener
"Here's the thing:" · "Let me be clear." · "I'll be honest." · "The uncomfortable truth is."

Cut it and start at the point. Then check you have not opened the next paragraph with a fresh one.

### Faux-insight setup
"What most teams get wrong is…" · "Here's what nobody tells you." · "The part everyone misses."

Flatters the writer as the only person who sees it. Cut the setup; let the claim stand alone.

### Rhetorical setup
"What if I told you…" · "Think about it:" · "Plot twist:" · a question the writer immediately answers.

### Mic-drop ending
"The future of work isn't coming. It's already in your inbox."

The final line that reaches for profundity — an aphorism, a metaphor, a reversal.

**Delete it. Do not rewrite it into a better one, and do not preserve the rhythm.** An unaided edit will polish this line rather than cut it, because it feels like the strongest thing in the piece. End on the clearest concrete sentence already in the draft, or on a plain next action.

### Summary ending
"In conclusion…" · "Ultimately…" · a last paragraph restating the piece.

The reader was just there. End on the last real point. **Cutting the signpost is not cutting the ending** — if the paragraph still restates the piece, it is still a summary ending.

### Trailing "-ing" clause
"…, underscoring their commitment to quality." · "…, reflecting its continued relevance."

Fact, comma, participle editorialising on the fact. The most reliable syntactic tell in the language. Cut the clause; the fact was fine.

### Interpretive aside
"That last part matters more than it sounds." · "The key point here is…" · "As you can see."

Stepping outside the subject to tell the reader how much weight to give it. If the point is clear, delete. If it is not, add a fact rather than an instruction.

## A tell wraps a point. Remove the wrapper, keep the point.

The most common way this skill goes wrong is deleting the whole sentence because its shape was wrong, and losing what it said.

"The detail that changes everything: nobody owns it" is a colon reveal wrapped around a real claim. The claim stays. Only the wrapper goes.

**The edit is finished when the draft still:**

- makes the same argument, in the same order
- keeps every true thing it said
- sounds like the same person wrote it
- is close to the same length — unless a passage was genuinely empty, and then your note names which passage and why the piece still works without it

**Cutting is not the score.** A draft that comes back at a quarter of its length has almost certainly lost content along with tells. If what remains reads like notes rather than the piece it was, you have overshot — put the points back in plain sentences.

## The swap test

**Swap the company name, the product and the country. If the sentence still works, it was never about this subject.**

The fastest single check in this skill, and it catches things no list will. "Our platform serves as the backbone of a more robust adoption process" passes every grammar check and belongs to nobody. Replace it with a fact, a mechanism, a consequence, or a judgement that could only be about this subject — or cut it.

## Sentence-level tells

A careful edit usually catches these unaided, so do not spend the review here. Sweep and move on.

- **Kill words** — delve, leverage, robust, seamless, elevate, unlock, empower, streamline, intricate, paradigm shift, game-changer, transformative, harness, ever-evolving, landscape, ecosystem.
- **Copula avoidance** — "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "represents", "acts as", where "is" or "has" was the word.
- **Importance puffery** — "marks a pivotal moment", "stands as a testament", "plays a vital role", "solidifies its position".
- **Weasel attribution** — "studies show", "experts agree", "many argue", "widely regarded as". Name the source or cut the claim. Never invent one; ask.
- **Empty adverbs** — just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally, importantly, crucially. Cut when they add nothing; **keep them when they carry the writer's real emphasis or uncertainty.**
- **Weak verb phrases** — "made a decision" → "decided". "Has the ability to" → "can".
- **Synonym cycling** — renaming one thing each clause to avoid repeating a word. Pick the clear word and repeat it.
- **Em dashes** — in short copy, none. In a longer piece, one or two, where they clearly beat a comma or a full stop. Remove clusters.
- **Formatting** — emoji in headings, bold scattered mid-sentence, bullets where two sentences would read better, a heading over every two-sentence section.

## Residue — any single one rejects the draft

- Chat leftovers: "I hope this helps!", "Certainly!", "Would you like me to…", "You're absolutely right!"
- Compliance residue: "As an AI language model…", "to ensure it aligns with guidelines"
- Knowledge disclaimers: "as of my last update", "based on available information"
- Unfilled placeholders: `[Your Name]`, `[Company Name]`, `[link here]`
- Tool fingerprints in links or citations: `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `citeturn0search0`, `:contentReference[oaicite:0]`

These are not style problems. They are proof nobody read the text before sending it.

**And do not add your own.** Replacing `[Company Name]` with `[drop in a specific proof point here]` has not removed a placeholder — it has moved one. If the draft needs a fact you do not have, say so outside the draft, in your note.

## Facts are read, never remembered

Every date, price, name, figure and link comes from the source on this pass — not from earlier in the conversation, not from a previous draft, not from what you know about the subject.

**Never fill a missing fact from memory.** Recall is a hypothesis. If an input is missing, name it and stop. A confidently recalled wrong price is worse than a blank.

## The pass

1. **Read it whole.** Note the markers to protect.
2. **Read it aloud.** Most tells are audible before they are visible.
3. **Mark, do not fix.** Sweep the structural section first, then the rest.
4. **Count per passage.** Three or more means rewrite the passage.
5. **Rewrite the shapes.**
6. **Then check what you introduced.** Re-read your own version against the structural list. This step is not optional — an unaided edit introduces a new negation reframe, a new placeholder, or a new throat-clearing opener almost every time.
7. **Voice check.** Does it still sound like them? If it now sounds like careful nobody, you have overshot — put the character back.
8. **Report by tell, not line by line**, so the writer learns which shapes they reach for.

## If you are thinking this, you are keeping a tell

Every line in this table is something an unaided edit actually said while leaving a tell in place.

| The thought | What is true |
|---|---|
| "It's genuinely good — specific, sensory" | Said about an invented scene. Vivid and fabricated is still fabricated. |
| "It earns its place — left it alone" | Said about a mic-drop ending. Earning its place is the feeling every tell produces. |
| "Kept the closing rhythm" | Rhythm is the tell. Preserving it while changing the words changes nothing. |
| "I cut the signpost" | Cutting "In conclusion" while keeping the summary keeps the summary. |
| "The triplet gives it structure" | Three beats is cadence, not structure. Say it once. |
| "I replaced it with a better placeholder" | A placeholder is a placeholder. Put the gap in your note, not the draft. |
| "This scenario makes it relatable" | It makes it fiction. Open from something real. |
| "The reader needs a signpost" | Write the next point. |
| "Small edit, I'll skip the check" | Step 6 is where the new tells are. Skipping it is how the draft ends up where it started. |
| "The draft is much shorter — that's the honest size of what was left" | Usually false. Points went out with the shapes that carried them. Unwrap, don't bin. |
| "I collapsed it into one positive statement" | Check it. "Y, not X" is the same tell reversed, and this is the sentence it hides in. |
| "There wasn't much left once the tells came out" | If a piece is nothing but tells, say so and stop. Do not hand back a stub and call it edited. |

## Stop and start over when

- You deleted a tell and your replacement is on this list
- You kept a line because it was the best writing in the draft
- You wrote a scene that did not happen
- You told the reader what they did, felt or thought
- The text would work for another company by swapping the nouns
- It now reads well and sounds like nobody
- The draft came back at half its length or less
- Your replacement sentence names what the thing is not
